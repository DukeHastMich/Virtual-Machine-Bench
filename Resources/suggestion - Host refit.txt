Host Refit and Physical Machine-Construction Suggestions
=========================================================

Purpose
-------

The host is not merely a settings screen for a fixed emulator. It is the
construction and inspection environment for a virtual retro computer assembled
from modeled physical components.

The system should allow an operator to mix and match real historical hardware
models as closely as possible. It may later include Cromwell-branded hybrid and
novel devices, but those devices must remain explicit pieces of hardware with
documented behavior. They must not silently bypass electrical, mechanical, or
protocol constraints.

The intended result is:

* From inside the virtual machine, the installed hardware behaves like the
  asserted physical build.
* From the operator's chair, the host clearly communicates what machine has
  been constructed and how its components are connected.
* Every functioning connection is physically plausible or is made plausible by
  an explicit adapter or converter that could exist as real electronics.


Streamlined Hardware Changes
----------------------------

The final host may use dropdowns to make hardware installation and replacement
fast. This is a user-interface convenience only. Selecting an item must perform
the equivalent of a real hardware change and update the complete machine model.

A dropdown selection should therefore resolve and validate:

* The exact component model and revision.
* The physical slot, socket, bay, or mounting point.
* Bus and connector compatibility.
* Cable type, connector position, and orientation.
* Electrical signaling and voltage compatibility.
* Power requirements and available supply capacity.
* Jumper, switch, and termination settings.
* IRQ, DMA, I/O-port, ROM-address, and memory-window assignments.
* Firmware, option-ROM, driver, and CMOS support.
* Required adapters, brackets, converters, and auxiliary hardware.
* Conflicts or behavioral consequences introduced by the change.

The streamlined interface must never conceal a required physical component or
silently reinterpret an incompatible connection as compatible.


Machine Build Information Side Panel
------------------------------------

The host currently lacks a sufficiently clear informational side panel for the
active machine build. This panel is essential. It should act as a live build
sheet, inventory, wiring summary, and diagnostic reference.

At minimum, it should identify:

* Chassis manufacturer, model, revision, and form factor.
* Power supply model, rail capacities, connectors, and current load.
* Motherboard manufacturer, model, revision, and jumper configuration.
* Chipset and supporting motherboard logic.
* BIOS vendor, version, date, and installed option ROMs.
* CPU model, package, socket, clock speed, and turbo state.
* Numeric coprocessor model and clocking arrangement.
* Installed memory modules, organization, speed, parity, and bank layout.
* Every expansion card and its occupied ISA slot.
* Storage controllers and all attached drives.
* Inserted removable media and physical write-protect state.
* Video card, monitor, signal standard, cable, and conversion hardware.
* Keyboard, keyboard controller, protocol, cable, and adapter chain.
* Speaker, motherboard driver circuit, connector, and chassis mounting.
* Serial, parallel, network, and other external peripherals.
* IRQ, DMA, I/O-port, ROM-address, and memory-window allocation.
* Active jumpers, DIP switches, cable-select positions, and termination.
* Detected resource conflicts, unsupported combinations, and unsafe states.

Each component should also carry a visible classification:

* Historical original component.
* Historical compatible or third-party component.
* Cromwell reproduction.
* Cromwell hybrid.
* Cromwell novel or experimental device.

The operator should never have to infer the active machine from scattered menu
checkmarks. The panel must state exactly what physical machine the emulator is
asserting at that moment.


Core Physical-Connection Rule
-----------------------------

No logical compatibility should exist without a physically plausible
connection path.

Every connection should be represented as:

    source device
        -> source electrical and protocol interface
        -> source connector
        -> cable or backplane path
        -> optional adapter or converter
        -> destination connector
        -> destination electrical and protocol interface
        -> destination device

A shared informal name is not enough to establish compatibility. Two devices
described as RGB, IDE, serial, floppy, keyboard, or audio devices may use
different connectors, voltage levels, timing, synchronization, encoding, or
protocols.


Adapters and Converters Are Hardware
------------------------------------

An adapter must be a first-class component in the machine construction model.
It must not be reduced to a hidden compatibility flag.

The model should distinguish between:

* Passive pin or mechanical adapters.
* Cable wiring adapters.
* Voltage-level translators.
* Impedance or termination networks.
* Protocol converters.
* Active scan or timing converters.
* Intelligent bridges with firmware or buffering.
* Mounting brackets and enclosure modifications.

Each adapter should declare:

* Its real or Cromwell model identity.
* Input and output connectors.
* Supported electrical levels and protocols.
* Directionality.
* Power source and consumption.
* Timing limits, bandwidth, and latency.
* Resolution, color, frequency, or data-rate restrictions.
* Signal loss, distortion, or conversion artifacts.
* Required configuration and firmware.
* Historical availability or Cromwell classification.

For example, a digital RGB output must not silently connect to an incompatible
analog RGB display simply because both are called RGB. The construction must
include the appropriate real or plausible active conversion device. That device
must expose its physical connector chain, supported scan rates, voltage levels,
color conversion, synchronization behavior, latency, artifacts, and power
requirements. Conceptually, this is like requiring the correct barrel connector
and conversion electronics instead of treating unlike interfaces as identical.


Compatibility and Failure States
--------------------------------

The construction validator should report more than compatible or incompatible.
Useful states include:

Physically direct
    The devices have a documented native connection and can be connected with
    the selected cable or backplane path.

Adapted
    The connection is valid only through the explicitly listed adapter,
    converter, bridge, bracket, or cable.

Resource conflict
    The hardware can connect physically, but IRQ, DMA, I/O, ROM, memory, or bus
    ownership conflicts prevent correct operation.

Firmware unsupported
    The hardware is electrically connected, but the selected BIOS, option ROM,
    CMOS implementation, or device firmware cannot configure or operate it.

Driver unsupported
    The machine can enumerate or electrically access the hardware, but the
    selected guest software lacks the required driver.

Electrically unsafe
    Connectors may fit or be adaptable, but voltage, current, impedance, signal
    direction, or power requirements are incompatible.

Mechanically incompatible
    A bracket, mounting adapter, enclosure modification, cable clearance, or
    different chassis is required.

Historically unavailable
    The connection is physically plausible but the component or adapter falls
    outside the selected historical date or provenance constraints.

Cromwell experimental
    The device is intentionally hybrid or novel. Its invented circuitry and
    deviations from historical hardware are explicitly documented.

An invalid build may be allowed as an educational experiment where safe, but it
must not function as though the missing electronics existed. The host must show
the failure state and the guest must observe the resulting authentic absence,
conflict, malfunction, or inability to initialize.


Connection Rules Across Major Subsystems
----------------------------------------

Floppy storage
    Drive type, controller capability, data rate, cable position, twist, drive
    select, density support, BIOS support, CMOS type, power connector, and media
    type must agree. A 2.88 MB drive requires the asserted controller, drive,
    firmware support, data rate, and physical connection path.

IDE storage
    Controller channel, cable type and length, connector position, master/slave
    or cable-select jumpers, BIOS capacity support, power, and device timing must
    be represented.

ISA expansion cards
    Bus width, keying, slot position, clock tolerance, IRQ, DMA, I/O ports, ROM
    address, memory aperture, jumpers, and option-ROM requirements must be
    validated.

Video and displays
    Connector, analog or digital signaling, voltage levels, sync polarity,
    horizontal and vertical scan ranges, pixel clock, color encoding, cable,
    termination, and adapters must form a valid chain.

Keyboard and pointing devices
    Connector shape alone is insufficient. Protocol, voltage, clock/data
    behavior, controller support, passive adapters, and active converters must
    be distinguished.

Audio and PC speaker
    Logic source, motherboard driver circuit, voltage, impedance, connector,
    speaker part, enclosure mounting, and any external amplification or adapter
    should be explicit.

Memory
    Package, socket, data width, parity organization, speed, voltage, bank
    population rules, chipset addressing, and motherboard limits must agree.

Power
    Connector type, pinout, rail voltage, current demand, sequencing, and total
    supply capacity should be validated. A mechanical fit does not imply safe
    electrical compatibility.


Inspecting a Connection Chain
-----------------------------

The operator should be able to select any component in the side panel and see
its complete connection path. For example:

    Drive B:
      Toshiba 2.88 MB ED drive
        -> 34-pin drive interface
        -> ribbon cable, second connector
        -> period-compatible or Cromwell ISA floppy controller
        -> 16-bit ISA slot 4
        -> I/O 03F0h-03F7h, IRQ 6, DMA 2
        -> 1 Mbps ED transfer capability
        -> BIOS extension enabled
        -> CMOS drive type: 2.88 MB
        -> power connector and rail load shown

The display should make missing links and conflicts visually obvious. It should
also explain why a proposed device cannot be installed or what additional part
is required.


Sneaker Net Boundary
--------------------

Sneaker Net is a host-side media preparation and transport system. It is
analogous to preparing a disk on another computer and carrying it to the
virtual machine. Its host interface does not need to resemble software running
inside the selected retro machine.

The archaeological boundary begins when the operator asserts or inserts the
created medium into the machine build.

Sneaker Net may therefore create any supported external medium, including a
2.88 MB disk. The receiving build determines whether that medium can actually be
inserted, detected, read, written, or rejected. A valid 2.88 MB configuration is
possible when the operator has explicitly installed a suitable drive,
controller, cabling, firmware support, CMOS configuration, and other required
hardware.

The receiving drive model should govern:

* Whether the medium physically fits.
* Density and media sensing.
* Supported controller data rates.
* Disk-change behavior.
* Write-protect state.
* Motor, readiness, seek, and rotational timing.
* Errors caused by unsupported or mismatched media.

Sneaker Net must not silently upgrade the receiving machine merely because the
operator created a higher-capacity image.


Machine Manifest and Connection Graph
-------------------------------------

The active build should be stored as a structured machine manifest plus a
connection graph. This should become the single source of truth for:

* Hardware construction and dropdown choices.
* Host side-panel presentation.
* Emulator device creation and wiring.
* Resource and compatibility validation.
* Jumpers, switches, CMOS values, and option-ROM loading.
* Save states and reproducible machine builds.
* Diagnostics and guest-visible hardware reports.
* Historical provenance and documentation.
* Cromwell hybrid and novel-device specifications.

The manifest should identify exact component models and revisions rather than
only broad device classes. The connection graph should identify every slot,
socket, port, cable, connector, adapter, converter, and power path.

A saved build should be reproducible. Reloading it should reconstruct the same
hardware inventory, wiring, resource allocation, firmware, adapter chain, and
guest-visible behavior.


Recommended Host Refit Order
----------------------------

1. Add the machine build informational side panel.
2. Define stable component identities and classifications.
3. Create the structured machine manifest.
4. Represent slots, ports, connectors, cables, and power paths explicitly.
5. Add the connection and compatibility graph.
6. Make adapters and converters first-class components.
7. Route dropdown hardware changes through the manifest and validator.
8. Display conflicts, missing adapters, and unsupported firmware clearly.
9. Make emulator device construction derive from the validated manifest.
10. Extend the same framework to documented Cromwell hybrid and novel devices.

The side panel should arrive early because it makes every subsequent hardware
change visible and auditable. It is not cosmetic. Without it, a streamlined
dropdown can change the asserted machine without leaving a clear physical
account of what machine now exists.


Guiding Principle
-----------------

Convenient host controls are encouraged, but convenience must operate by
constructing a valid machine rather than bypassing one.

If two components cannot communicate through real connectors, signaling,
protocols, power, firmware, and any required adapters, they must not communicate
inside the virtual machine. When Cromwell invents a new bridge or hybrid device,
that bridge must be documented and modeled as actual electronics occupying a
real place in the build.


Addendum: Socketed and Flashable OEM Firmware
---------------------------------------------

The construction system must support authentic OEM BIOS images, including
firmware that can be programmed or updated through the mechanisms provided by
the asserted motherboard or adapter. BIOS contents must not be treated as a
permanent emulator resource selected independently of the physical machine.

Firmware storage should be represented as real hardware components, such as:

* Mask ROM.
* Socketed EPROM or EEPROM.
* Flash EEPROM.
* Separate even/odd ROM pairs where used.
* Motherboard system BIOS devices.
* Video, storage-controller, network, and other option ROMs.
* Auxiliary keyboard-controller, microcontroller, or device firmware where it
  is replaceable or programmable on the reference hardware.

Each firmware device or socket should specify:

* Manufacturer and exact device type.
* Capacity, organization, data width, and number of chips.
* Pinout, package, socket, and required adapter where applicable.
* Address decoding and guest-visible mapping.
* Erase-block and programming granularity.
* Programming voltage and write-enable requirements.
* Read, program, and erase timing.
* Hardware and software write-protection mechanisms.
* Boot-block or recovery-region protection.
* Supported OEM image sizes, interleaving, checksums, and signatures.
* Compatibility with the selected motherboard or expansion-card revision.
* Persistent contents and provenance.

If the selected motherboard supports in-system flashing, authentic OEM flash
utilities running inside the guest should be able to identify, erase, program,
verify, and protect the emulated flash device through the original chipset and
firmware interfaces. The host must not intercept a recognized update and simply
replace a file. Guest software should exercise the same command sequences,
write-enables, chipset gates, and reset requirements used by the real hardware.

Programming must reproduce relevant physical behavior and failure conditions:

* Writes fail while hardware or chipset protection is active.
* Unsupported device-identification commands return authentic values or fail.
* Incorrect programming voltage or incompatible algorithms do not succeed.
* Erase and programming operations require modeled time.
* Reads during programming follow the selected flash device's documented
  status-polling behavior.
* Reset or power interruption during an update can leave incomplete contents.
* A corrupted main BIOS can prevent POST or boot.
* A protected boot block or documented recovery mechanism works only when the
  asserted hardware actually provides it.
* Recovery may require a jumper, switch, recovery disk, external programmer, or
  replacement ROM, just as on the physical machine.

Firmware contents must persist across power cycles as part of the component's
nonvolatile state. Save states must not accidentally roll flash contents backward
unless the selected preservation policy explicitly snapshots nonvolatile media.
The host should keep recoverable backups for operator safety, but those backups
must remain outside guest-visible hardware and must not silently repair a failed
flash operation.

The machine build side panel should display:

* Installed BIOS and option-ROM device models.
* OEM, product, version, date, size, and image hash.
* Socket or board location and address range.
* Original, replacement, modified, or Cromwell firmware classification.
* Current write-protect, recovery-jumper, and boot-block state.
* Whether in-system programming is physically supported.
* Unsaved or changed nonvolatile contents.
* Compatibility or checksum warnings without concealing the installed image.

The connection validator must treat an OEM BIOS image as compatible only when
its electrical organization, capacity, mapping, chipset expectations, board
revision, and required peripheral hardware agree with the selected build. If an
image requires two physical ROMs, an interposer, a larger decoder, or a flash
upgrade module, those parts must appear explicitly in the connection graph.

Host-side installation should support operations analogous to real service work:

* Insert or replace a socketed ROM while power is off.
* Install an approved flash-upgrade module or adapter.
* Load an image into an external programmer and then install the programmed
  device.
* Operate a physical recovery or write-enable jumper.
* Preserve and restore ROM dumps as external archival actions.

Cromwell hybrid or novel firmware devices may offer new capabilities, but the
device, programming interface, adapter, and deviations from original hardware
must be documented. They must not cause an original OEM motherboard to acquire
flash support without an asserted physical modification that could implement it.
